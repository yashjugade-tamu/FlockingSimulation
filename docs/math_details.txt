AFTER THE RAIN: STREETLIGHT SWARM
Math in Simple Language

This note explains the main math in src/main.cpp. The numbers are simulation
units, not meters. In the ground plane, 55 screen pixels equal one world unit.
World x goes along the street, y is height, and z goes across the street.

1. WHAT EACH MOTH KNOWS

Each moth keeps track of where it is (position), which way it is moving
(velocity), and how quickly its motion is changing (acceleration). Every frame,
it looks at nearby moths, lights, and buses to decide how to change its motion.

2. HOW THE FLOCK MOVES

The program counts another moth as a neighbor when it is within 2.35 units.
Each moth uses three simple ideas:

Separation: do not crowd nearby moths. Within 0.52 units, a moth adds a push
away from each neighbor. A closer neighbor creates a stronger push:

    push away = direction away from neighbor / distance

Alignment: turn toward the average direction of nearby moths. The code first
averages their velocities, then steers toward that average direction.

Cohesion: stay with the group. The code finds the average position of nearby
moths, then steers toward it.

For alignment and cohesion, “steer toward” means: choose a desired velocity,
compare it with the moth's current velocity, and use the difference as an
acceleration. The maximum desired speed is 2.25 units per second. The three
rules are added together. Separation is multiplied by 1.8 * 1.6, alignment by
0.8 * 1.15, and cohesion by 0.65. These weights set how strongly each rule
matters. If a moth has no neighbors, alignment and cohesion do nothing.

3. HOW LIGHT ATTRACTS MOTHS

In cycle mode, one lamp turns on at a time, moving from the first lamp to the
last and then starting over. In manual mode, you choose which lamps are on.
In both modes, moths are attracted to lamps that are on. No Attraction mode
turns off this pull.

The light pull gets weaker with distance. A lamp only affects a moth when it is
between 1 and 9.5 units away. For a lamp at distance r, its influence is

    influence = (1 - r / 9.5) / (1 + r / 2.8)

This value gets smaller as r gets larger. The moth combines the directions to
the active lamps, giving more weight to lamps with greater influence, then
steers toward the combined direction. The steering tries to bring the moth
toward the lamp, but does not make it instantly jump there.

4. WIND AND STEERING LIMITS

Wind gently pushes moths along the street. It tries to bring their sideways
speed toward 12/55 units per second. The wind force is deliberately small.

The flock, light, wind, and obstacle accelerations are added together. If the
total acceleration gets larger than 5.8 units per second squared, the program
shortens it while keeping its direction. Moth speed is also capped at 2.25
units per second.

5. HOW MOTHS AVOID BUSES

Before moving, the program predicts where each moth and bus will be at six
points over the next second. A moth's prediction follows its current velocity.
A bus's prediction follows its sine-wave lane motion, including its future
direction and speed. This can reveal paths that will cross even when a bus is
not close to the moth right now. For steering, the bus box is enlarged by 0.9
units on its sides and above the roof. This gives a moth room to turn early.

If the future point is inside or very close to this enlarged box, the moth
steers away from the nearest part of the box. This steering force is scaled by
3.4 * 1.4, then added to its flocking and other forces. The desired escape
velocity includes the bus's predicted velocity, so the moth steers away from
where the bus is moving.

There is also a safety check after each movement step. If a moth's center or
wings end up inside the bus safety box (expanded by 0.45 units), the program
moves it out through the nearest side or
above the roof and gives it a small velocity pointing outward. Moths cannot
escape underneath the bus because the street is their lower boundary. This
second check is a custom safeguard for cases where steering alone is not fast
enough, including crowded flocks.

The book discusses obstacle avoidance using potential fields and steering in
Chapter 5, and steering around obstacles in flocking in Chapter 6. This project
uses those general ideas, with its own bus-box steering and overlap safeguard.

6. HOW THE MOTHS MOVE EACH FRAME

The program uses semi-implicit Euler integration. In simple terms, it first
changes a moth's velocity using acceleration, then moves it using that updated
velocity:

    new velocity = old velocity + acceleration * time step
    new position = old position + new velocity * time step

The time step is the small amount of time for one update (at most 0.025
seconds). This method is simple and works well for this loose, gently changing
motion. It is the update order shown in Equation 2.4 of House and Keyser.

Explicit Euler is slightly different: it moves the moth using its old velocity
before acceleration changes the velocity. This program does not use that
position update.

After moving, moths bounce off the height and street-depth limits. At the left
and right ends of the flight area, they wrap to the other side. In No Attraction
mode, moths stay at or below 3.0 units high so they continue flying near the
street and buses. In the light-attraction modes, they can rise as high as 5.2
units toward the lamps.

7. HOW THE SPATIAL GRID HELPS

The program divides the space into small 2-unit boxes. Each moth is stored in
the box where it is flying. To find neighbors, it checks only boxes near that
moth, then measures the exact distance to the possible neighbors. This saves
work when there are many moths; it does not change how the flocking rules work.

8. BOOK REFERENCE

Donald H. House and John C. Keyser, Foundations of Physically Based Modeling
and Animation, CRC Press, 2017. Relevant topics include Euler updates
(Chapter 2, Equation 2.4), collision avoidance (Chapter 5, Section 5.3), and
flocking (Chapter 6, including Section 6.5.4 on obstacle steering).
